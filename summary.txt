1. THE PITCH
================================================================================

The situation
  A hospital or a paper trains a model that predicts who has, or will get, heart
  disease. Alongside each prediction it shows an "explanation": the three or four
  things about this patient that pushed the risk up or down the most (age, blood
  pressure, smoking...). The explanation is what makes people trust the model. It
  is usually produced by a method called SHAP, and papers usually justify it by
  saying "the important features make clinical sense".

The question nobody was measuring
  If we trained that same model again - on a slightly different sample of patients
  from the same population, or with a different random seed, or as a different
  kind of model with the same accuracy - would THIS patient get the SAME
  explanation? If not, the explanation is describing an accident of how the model
  was built, not the patient.

What we did
  We took two public heart-disease datasets, four standard kinds of model and five
  standard explanation methods. We trained each model hundreds of times (with
  different seeds, and on re-drawn training samples), and each time explained the
  SAME 500 patients, who were never used for training. Then we measured, patient by
  patient, how much the explanation moved, and which change moved it most.

What we found
  1. The explanations are honest about the model ("faithful"): changing the three
     features an explanation ranks highest moves the prediction 2.2 to 3.3 times
     more than changing three random features.
  2. The explanations look medically sensible ("plausible"): they put weight on
     age, blood pressure and smoking, matching a published risk equation.
  3. BUT on the small dataset the explanation for one patient is not reproducible.
     Train the model again on a re-drawn sample of the same size and a patient's
     top three features overlap only 0.52 to 0.70 (1 = identical, chance = 0.13).
     Changing only the random seed gives 0.82 to 0.90 - so the usual "we checked
     across seeds" test misses most of the problem.
  4. The cause is the amount of data. Cut the large dataset down to the small
     one's size and the same instability appears, while accuracy barely changes
     (AUC 0.83 to 0.85). Accuracy does not warn you.
  5. Which MODEL you choose changes a patient's explanation more than which
     EXPLANATION METHOD you choose.
  6. Settings that are equally good by cross-validation give different
     explanations too. For the neural network the setting responsible is weight
     decay.
  7. A popular trick for imbalanced data (SMOTE) makes the model over-predict risk
     by 1.6 to 3.6 times and changes the explanations.
  8. Three software traps were found that silently distort this kind of study.

Why it is believable
  - The plan was written and frozen (a tagged version in git) before any of the
    reliability numbers were computed.
  - Every explanation method was checked against the exact mathematical answer.
  - The whole analysis was repeated under five other "master seeds" (new split of
    the data, new patients, new everything) and the conclusions held every time.
  - Every setting that was our own choice was changed to see if it mattered.
  - The large dataset was rebuilt from the government's raw file and matched the
    public copy exactly, cell for cell.
  - Findings that go against us are reported, not hidden (Section 10).

One sentence
  For these heart-disease models, an individual patient's explanation is faithful
  and plausible but, on small data, not reproducible - and the checks people
  normally report (accuracy, a seed test, "it makes clinical sense") do not reveal
  that.

2. WORDS YOU NEED
================================================================================

Model            A formula learned from data that turns a patient's measurements
                 into a predicted risk.
Feature /        One measurement about a patient (age, systolic blood pressure,
predictor        smoker yes/no ...).
Training set     The patients the model learns from.
Test set         Patients held back, used only to check the model.
AUC              A score for how well a model separates sick from healthy people.
                 0.5 = coin toss, 1.0 = perfect. Around 0.70 is typical for
                 Framingham-type data; 0.85 for the survey data.
Calibration      Whether predicted risks match reality (if the model says 20%, do
                 about 20 in 100 such patients get the disease?).
Explanation /    For one patient, a number per feature saying how much that
attribution      feature pushed this patient's prediction up or down.
SHAP             The most used way to compute attributions. It shares the
                 prediction out among the features fairly, using a rule from game
                 theory (Shapley values).
TreeSHAP,        Exact, fast versions of SHAP for tree models and for linear
linear SHAP      models.
KernelSHAP       A slower, approximate SHAP that works for any model.
Integrated /     Attribution methods for neural networks that use the model's
Expected         gradients. Expected Gradients is close to SHAP; Integrated
Gradients        Gradients with one reference point computes something different.
Top-3            The three features with the largest attribution for a patient.
Jaccard overlap  How much two top-3 lists share: (items in both) / (items in
                 either). 1 = same three, 0.5 = two shared, 0.2 = one shared,
                 0 = none shared.
Chance level     The overlap two RANDOM top-3 lists would have: 0.129 with 15
                 features, 0.090 with 21. Scores must be read against it.
Seed             The number that starts the random-number generator. Changing it
                 changes random choices inside training, nothing else.
Bootstrap        Re-drawing the training set: pick the same number of patients at
                 random WITH replacement. It imitates "what if we had collected a
                 slightly different sample from the same population".
Faithful         The explanation reflects what the model actually computes.
Plausible        The explanation agrees with what experts already believe.
Reproducible /   The explanation stays the same when something that should not
stable           matter (seed, sample, noise) is changed.
Multiplicity     Many different models fit the data equally well but disagree on
                 individuals.
Events per       Number of sick patients in the training set divided by the number
predictor        of features. The standard yardstick for "is there enough data".
ICC              Intraclass correlation. Here: of all the variation in
                 explanations, the share that is due to patients being different
                 from one another. Near 1 = the disturbance hardly matters.
SMOTE            A way to "balance" data by inventing synthetic sick patients.
Hyperparameter   A setting chosen before training (tree depth, learning rate ...).
Cross-validation Splitting the training set into parts to choose hyperparameters
                 without touching the test set.
Master seed      Our own term: one number that controls the train/test split, which
                 500 patients are explained, the background rows, the tuning folds
                 and every model seed. Changing it re-does the entire study.
Post hoc         Added after the plan was frozen. Such results are weaker evidence
                 and are labelled as such everywhere.

================================================================================
3. WHERE THE PROBLEM CAME FROM
================================================================================

3.1 The starting point: an earlier draft
  The project began with a draft paper by the author, "Evaluating the Reliability
  and Clinical Consistency of Explainability Methods in Deep Learning-Based Heart
  Disease Prediction". It trained three models with SMOTE, explained them with SHAP
  and Integrated Gradients, and ran three tests: stability under 1/2/5% noise on 30
  patients, agreement across 10 seeds, and agreement (Kendall's tau) with a ranking
  of risk factors built by hand from the ACC/AHA 2019 guideline. It ended in
  verdicts such as "TRUSTWORTHY" or "DO NOT USE". The author felt it was incomplete
  and asked for an honest assessment.

3.2 What a review of the draft found (eleven problems)
   1. The "clinical truth" was built from guideline recommendation classes. Those
      classes say how strong the evidence is for TREATING a factor, not how much the
      factor predicts disease. So the yardstick measured the wrong thing.
   2. Kendall's tau on 15 features with 8 ties, no confidence interval, no chance
      level. One verdict depended on 0.395 against a cut-off of 0.40.
   3. "Approximate SHAP beats exact SHAP" compared a random forest explained one way
      with a neural network explained another way: two things changed at once.
   4. A claimed "dissociation" compared a whole-population ranking with per-patient
      rankings: different levels, not comparable.
   5. Counterfactual explanations were announced but never produced.
   6. "Pre-registered" without a registration; one threshold changed after seeing
      results.
   7. The seed test ran on one dataset but was said to replicate on both.
   8. The stability metric averaged rank shifts over all features, so unimportant
      features at the bottom dominated it.
   9. Agreement with logistic regression was treated as agreement with the truth.
  10. Weak models, no XGBoost, one split, SMOTE never questioned.
  11. No test at all of whether explanations reflect the model (faithfulness).

3.3 What the literature said
  A survey of about 230 works (Literature_Survey.txt) showed three separate bodies
  of research that had not been joined for heart-disease tabular models:
   - Explanation research: explanations from different methods disagree, and can
     change between runs.
   - Clinical prediction research: models built on small samples give unstable
     predictions for individuals even when overall accuracy looks fine, and the
     recommended way to expose this is to bootstrap the whole model-building
     process.
   - One cardiology study showed SHAP output depends on the model and on
     correlated features.
  Each ingredient existed. What was missing was a single study that measured, on
  the same patients and under one plan, HOW MUCH each influence matters, and tied
  explanation stability to the amount of data.

3.4 How that became the problem statement
  The draft asked "which explanation method is trustworthy?" and answered with
  pass/fail labels. The review showed that question cannot be answered the way the
  draft tried: there is no agreed clinical truth to grade against, and a label
  hides the size of the effect. The useful question is one that can be measured
  without knowing the truth: does the explanation stay the same when things that
  should not matter are changed? That needs no clinician and no gold standard,
  only careful repetition. That is the question of this study.

================================================================================
4. THE QUESTION, STATED EXACTLY
================================================================================

  For a heart-disease risk model, how much of one patient's feature-attribution
  explanation is determined by the patient, and how much changes with
     (a) the kind of model,
     (b) the explanation method,
     (c) the training sample,
     (d) the random seed,
     (e) ordinary measurement noise in the patient's own values,
     (f) the explainer's background sample,
  and (added later) (g) the choice among equally good hyperparameter settings?

  Two properties are kept strictly apart throughout, because mixing them was the
  draft's central confusion:
     Faithfulness = does the explanation reflect what the model computes?
     Plausibility = does it agree with what experts expect?
  An explanation can be plausible without being faithful, and neither says
  anything about whether it is reproducible.

  What the study is NOT: it proposes no new model and no new explanation method,
  involves no clinicians, and validates no clinical tool. It is a methods audit on
  public data.

================================================================================
5. THE MATERIAL
================================================================================

5.1 Framingham teaching file
  4,240 people, 15 features, outcome = coronary heart disease within 10 years
  (644 cases, 15.2%). Seven features have missing values; glucose is missing for
  388 people (9.2%). No HDL cholesterol. No formal codebook.

5.2 BRFSS 2015 heart-disease extract
  253,680 survey respondents, 21 features, outcome = self-reported existing
  coronary heart disease or heart attack (23,893 cases, 9.42%). No missing values.
  A telephone survey at one point in time.

5.3 Why these two, and why only two
  - They are the two datasets of the original draft, and the two most used in the
    applied papers this audit is about. An audit of common practice should use the
    data that practice uses.
  - They differ in exactly the way that matters for the main finding: Framingham
    is SMALL (about 34 events per predictor in the training set), BRFSS is LARGE
    (about 910). One small and one large dataset lets the same pipeline show what
    data volume does.
  - They also differ in kind (a cohort predicting future disease vs a survey of
    existing disease), so a result that holds on both is less likely to be a quirk
    of one file.
  - Both are public, so anyone can re-run everything.
  - Only two because of time, agreed with the author. This is a stated limit.

5.4 Checking the data before trusting it (Phase 1)
  Reason: if the data are wrong, everything built on them is wrong, and an
  explanation study has no later step that would reveal it.
  What was checked: file fingerprint (SHA-256), size, case counts, the coding and
  range of every column, impossible values, missing values, duplicate rows; then a
  plain logistic regression to see that known risk factors (age, blood pressure,
  smoking, diabetes) come out with the expected direction.
  A stop rule was written down in advance: stop if a major risk factor has the
  wrong sign with certainty, or the AUC is outside a stated range.
  Result: passed. Framingham AUC 0.702, BRFSS 0.847.
  Unexpected: BRFSS has 23,899 exact duplicate rows. Among them disease prevalence
  is 0.9%, against 10.8% in the rest, which fits healthy people giving identical
  answers to a short questionnaire, not copying errors. They were kept.
  Later (post hoc): the BRFSS file was rebuilt from the CDC's raw 2015 file
  (441,456 records) by re-implementing the cleaning steps its author documented.
  The rebuilt file matched the public one exactly: 253,680 rows, 23,893 cases,
  23,899 duplicates, zero differing cells (results/brfss/cdc_check_report.txt).

6. THE METHOD, DECISION BY DECISION, WITH REASONS
================================================================================
Each decision below also has an entry in Decision_Register.txt (D-01 to D-46) with
the alternatives considered and the literature behind it.

6.1 Freeze the plan before computing any reliability number
  What : protocol, decision register and code committed and tagged in git
         (protocol-v1.0) before any model was retrained or any score computed.
  Why  : a study like this has hundreds of small choices. If they are made after
         seeing results, it is impossible - even for the author - to know whether
         a finding is real or was steered. Freezing first removes that doubt.
         Everything added later is labelled "post hoc" wherever it appears.

6.2 One fixed train/test split and one fixed set of 500 explained patients
  What : 80% train, 20% test, stratified by outcome. From the test set, 500
         patients drawn once and used for every model.
  Why  : the whole study compares the explanation of THE SAME patient across
         hundreds of models. That is only possible if those patients are fixed and
         were never in any training set. Repeated splitting (cross-validation)
         would be better for estimating accuracy but would put the explained
         patients into training sets. Accuracy is not what is being measured.
  Cost : results are conditional on one split. Answered later by re-running the
         whole study under other master seeds (Section 8.14).
  Why 500: a compute budget. Checked: splitting them into two halves of 250
         changes the main numbers by at most 0.03.

6.3 No rebalancing of the classes in the main analysis
  What : models are trained on the data as they are (15% / 9% cases).
  Why  : the quantity of interest is a predicted RISK. Inventing extra sick
         patients (SMOTE) teaches the model that disease is far more common than it
         is, so its risks come out too high. An explanation of such a model
         explains a distorted model. SMOTE is studied separately as its own
         experiment (6.16) so its effect can be shown instead of assumed.

6.4 Missing values: fill with the training median (or the most common value)
  Why  : (i) the same rule can be applied to a new patient at prediction time,
         which is what a deployed model must do; (ii) all four models then receive
         identical inputs, so differences between models cannot come from
         different handling of missing data. The filling rule is learned only from
         the training rows, again inside every retrain, so no information leaks
         from test patients.
  Checked: using a regression-based filling instead, and using only patients with
         nothing missing, changes nothing material.

6.5 Four kinds of model
  Logistic regression (LR), random forest (RF), XGBoost (XGB), a small neural
  network (MLP, two hidden layers of 64 and 32 units).
  Why these: the study asks whether the KIND of model changes the explanation, so
         it needs kinds that work differently. LR is the standard method in
         clinical risk prediction and the natural reference. RF and XGB are the
         tree-ensemble methods that applied heart-disease papers actually use, and
         they build predictions from yes/no splits, not a smooth formula. The MLP
         is needed because the gradient-based explanation methods only work on a
         smooth, differentiable model. Together they cover linear, tree and neural
         behaviour.
  Not included: newer deep tabular models and "interpretable by design" models.
         Stated as a limit.

6.6 Hyperparameters tuned once per dataset, by 5-fold cross-validated log loss
  Why log loss: it penalises wrong probabilities, and probabilities are what a
         risk model outputs. Tuning on AUC would ignore calibration.
  Why once (not inside every retrain): the retrains are meant to isolate ONE
         change at a time (only the seed, or only the sample). If the settings
         also changed in each retrain, two things would move together.
  Risk : maybe tuning once hides instability. Tested: re-tuning inside every
         bootstrap, done correctly, changes agreement by at most 0.05 (Framingham)
         and 0.015 (BRFSS).
  Honest basis of the search ranges and fixed settings: they are common defaults
         or were limited by compute, not derived from theory. So each was changed
         to see whether it matters (Sections 8.11 and 8.15).

6.7 Three kinds of retraining
  Base      : the full training set, seed 42.
  Seed      : same data, seeds 0 to 9 (RF, XGB, MLP; LR has no randomness).
  Bootstrap : training rows re-drawn with replacement, model seed kept fixed;
              100 times for Framingham, 50 for BRFSS.
  Why both seed and bootstrap: they answer different questions. A seed change
         asks "is the training procedure itself jittery?". A bootstrap asks "would
         we get the same thing from another sample of the same population?" -
         which is the question that matters for trusting an explanation, because
         the particular patients who ended up in a training set are an accident.
         Papers usually report only the seed test. Showing the two side by side
         is the core comparison of the study.
  Why the bootstrap re-does preprocessing too: the instability of a model
         includes the instability of everything fitted from the data.
  Why 100 / 50: compute. Checked: half as many gives the same numbers to within
         0.02.

6.8 The explanation methods, and why each model gets a "native" one
  LR  : exact linear SHAP (a formula; no approximation).
  RF  : TreeSHAP.
  XGB : TreeSHAP.
  MLP : Integrated Gradients and Expected Gradients.
  All four base models: also KernelSHAP.
  Why native methods: each is the exact or standard method for that model, so
         differences are not caused by a poor approximation.
  Why KernelSHAP as well: to separate "which model" from "which explainer", one of
         the two must be held fixed while the other changes. KernelSHAP works on
         every model and on one common scale (log-odds), so with it the explainer
         is fixed and only the model changes. Comparing KernelSHAP with the native
         method on the same model changes only the explainer.
  Why two methods for the MLP: Integrated Gradients with a single reference point
         is widely used but is not a Shapley value; Expected Gradients averages
         over many reference points and is close to one. Having both shows how
         much that distinction matters.

6.9 "Interventional" attributions with 100 real background rows
  What : to compute a feature's contribution, the method replaces other features
         with values taken from 100 real training patients.
  Why interventional: it answers "what did the MODEL use?". The alternative
         (conditional) answers "what do the DATA imply?" and can give credit to a
         feature the model never looks at, just because it is correlated with one
         it does. An audit of models needs the first.
  Why real rows, not an average patient: an "average patient" (0.4 of a smoker)
         does not exist; real rows keep the reference realistic.
  Why 100: it is the tree explainer's built-in limit, and cost grows with it.
         Checked with 50 and 200 rows and with fresh draws (Section 8.9) - and that
         check turned up a finding of its own.

6.10 Check every explainer against the exact answer
  What : for the Framingham models, the true Shapley values were computed by brute
         force - all 32,768 combinations of 15 features - and every explainer was
         compared with them. Also: do the attributions add up to the prediction?
         And: if the model is trained on scrambled labels, do the explanations
         change? (They should.)
  Why  : if the explainer is wrong, an "unstable explanation" might just be a
         buggy explainer. This step is what later caught the KernelSHAP default
         problem (Section 10).

6.11 The agreement measure: overlap of the top three features, with a chance level
  What : for one patient, take the top-3 features under explanation A and under
         explanation B; compute the Jaccard overlap. Report the average over
         patients, next to what two random rankings would score.
  Why top-3: a clinician or a patient reads the top few reasons, not a ranking of
         15. The draft's metric averaged over all features and was dominated by
         reshuffling among features nobody looks at.
  Why also other measures: top-1, top-5, rank correlation, rank-biased overlap and
         cosine similarity are all reported, so the result does not hang on one
         choice. It was the same under all of them.
  Why a chance level: without it a number like 0.5 cannot be judged.
  Why not only similarity of the raw numbers: two explanations can have very
         similar numbers and still order the top features differently. This was
         confirmed: similarity of raw attributions was 0.83-0.88 where the top-3
         agreed only 0.52-0.59.

6.12 Four axes, measured separately
  Faithfulness, robustness to measurement noise, reproducibility across
  retraining, plausibility.
  Why separately: the draft folded these into one verdict. They are different
         properties and an explanation can have some without others - which turned
         out to be the main finding.

6.13 Faithfulness test
  What : replace the patient's top-3 features with values from a random background
         row and record how far the prediction moves; do the same for three random
         features and for the bottom three.
  Why  : if the explanation is right about what matters, changing those features
         must move the prediction far more than changing arbitrary ones. Replacing
         with background values matches how the attributions themselves were
         defined, so the test is fair to them.

6.14 Plausibility reference: a published risk equation, not a guideline
  What : the D'Agostino 2008 general cardiovascular risk equation (inputs: age,
         sex, body-mass index, systolic blood pressure, treatment, smoking,
         diabetes). It was applied UNCHANGED to the Framingham file, then its own
         exact Shapley values on those same patients were computed. That gives an
         importance profile for a published, validated model, measured in exactly
         the same way as the models' explanations.
  Why  : the draft compared "how important a model thinks a feature is" with "how
         strongly a guideline recommends treating it" - two different quantities.
         A risk equation is a statement about prediction, the same kind of thing
         as the model. Applying the same Shapley machinery to it makes the
         comparison like for like.
  Safety check written in advance: if the equation did not discriminate on this
         file (AUC below 0.60) it would not be used. It scored 0.718.
  Why group features first: blood pressure appears as four columns (systolic,
         diastolic, hypertension flag, medication). A model can spread importance
         among them arbitrarily. Grouping them into "blood pressure" compares
         what matters medically.
  Why a "random assignment" comparison: with only six or seven groups, any two
         importance profiles look somewhat alike. So the score is always shown
         next to what a random shuffle scores (0.65-0.74).
  Second, independent reference: INTERHEART (a 52-country case-control study).
         Used as secondary only, because it shares just four groups with the data
         and measures lipids differently.
  For BRFSS no risk equation fits (it is a survey of existing disease), so the
         study reports where the attribution goes: established risk factors,
         likely consequences of disease, prior stroke, access/social items.

6.15 Measurement-noise test sized from published data
  What : each patient's values are re-drawn as if measured again, and the patient
         is re-explained.
         Framingham: blood pressure varies 8.6% between visits, cholesterol 5.95%,
         glucose 5.6%, heart rate 3 beats per minute - each from a published
         source.
         BRFSS: three self-report items are changed at rates set so that a
         simulated second interview reproduces published re-interview agreement.
  Why  : the draft used arbitrary 1%, 2%, 5% noise on everything. That says
         nothing about reality. The relevant question is: if this patient's blood
         pressure were simply taken again next week, would the explanation change?
         Only real measurement variation answers that.
  Items with no usable published figure (age, body-mass index, cigarettes, yes/no
         items) were left unchanged instead of guessing.

6.16 Further analyses
  Sources of variation (ICC): for each source in turn, how much of the variation
         in explanations comes from patients being different vs from that source.
         Why: it puts all sources on one scale so they can be ranked.
  SMOTE arm: retrain on SMOTE-balanced data, compare calibration and explanations.
         Why: SMOTE is routine in the audited papers, including the draft.
  Sample-size experiment: shrink BRFSS to Framingham's size and repeat.
         Why: Framingham and BRFSS differ in many ways. To show that SIZE is the
         cause, size must be changed while everything else stays the same.
  Per-patient associations: which patients have unstable explanations?
         Why: to look for a mechanism, not only an average.
  Hyperparameter analysis: among settings that cross-validation cannot tell apart,
         do explanations agree? Why: "which setting was picked" is one more
         accident of model building.
  Replication under other master seeds: redo everything with a new split.
         Why: the strongest available answer to "you got lucky with one split".

        - every setting chosen by us was varied, on both datasets;

================================================================================
8. THE RESULTS, ONE BY ONE
================================================================================
"Agreement" below always means the Jaccard overlap of a patient's top three
features, averaged over patients. Chance is 0.129 (Framingham) or 0.090 (BRFSS).

8.1 The models are ordinary, as they should be
                  Framingham AUC        BRFSS AUC
     LR           0.702                 0.847
     MLP          0.696                 0.849
     XGB          0.684                 0.851
     RF           0.665                 0.848
  On Framingham the simple model is best, which is what is expected with about 34
  events per predictor. On BRFSS all four are within 0.004 of each other.
  A published seven-input risk equation, with no training at all, scores 0.701 on
  the same test patients - as good as three of the four trained models (the random
  forest is slightly worse).
  Meaning: these are neither weak strawmen nor unusually strong models. They are
  what an applied paper would produce.

8.2 The explainers are numerically correct
  Against brute-force Shapley values: linear SHAP and XGB TreeSHAP exact; RF
  TreeSHAP within 0.28%; KernelSHAP within 0.5%; Expected Gradients within 4.2%;
  Integrated Gradients 15.6% off (it computes a different quantity, as expected).
  Models trained on scrambled labels gave explanations that agreed with the real
  ones at only 0.17-0.28.
  Meaning: what follows is not caused by broken explainers.

8.3 Faithful
  Replacing the top-3 features moved the prediction 2.2 to 3.0 times more than
  replacing three random features on Framingham and 3.0 to 3.3 times on BRFSS, in
  97-100% of patients. Replacing the bottom three moved it hardly at all.
  Meaning: the explanations do point at what the model uses.

8.4 Plausible
  Similarity with the risk-equation reference (1 = identical profile):
     LR 0.955, MLP 0.94, RF 0.909, XGB 0.900; a random shuffle scores 0.65-0.74.
  Every model ranks the same three groups first: age, blood pressure, smoking.
  Under three other ways of grouping the features the margin over a random shuffle
  stayed 0.17-0.28.
  On BRFSS 65-71% of attribution falls on established risk factors, 19-25% on
  things that are likely consequences of disease (general health, days of poor
  health, difficulty walking).
  Agreement with the independent INTERHEART reference was poor; the data lack the
  lipid measure INTERHEART ranks first.
  Meaning: the explanations look medically reasonable. This is the property papers
  usually stop at.

8.5 Not reproducible on small data  (the central result)
                              Framingham                BRFSS
                              seed     bootstrap        seed     bootstrap
     LR  linear SHAP          -        0.704            -        0.951
     XGB TreeSHAP             0.869    0.580            0.938    0.839
     MLP Expected Gradients   0.903    0.585            0.905    0.886
     MLP Integrated Gradients 0.848    0.528            0.903    0.884
     RF  TreeSHAP             0.815    0.517            0.947    0.857
  On Framingham 4% (LR) to 47% (RF) of patients have bootstrap agreement below 0.5,
  meaning two retrained models typically share at most two of the patient's top
  three reasons.
  How to read 0.52: on average two models trained on two samples of the same size
  from the same population agree on only about two of a patient's top three
  features, and for many patients fewer.
  Bootstrap agreement was lower than seed agreement in all 40 combinations of
  model, dataset and agreement measure.
  Three qualifications, all reported:
   - The DIRECTION is stable: a leading feature keeps its sign (raises or lowers
     risk) in 96-100% of retrains. What moves is the order.
   - Grouping correlated features before ranking does not remove the instability.
   - The whole-population ranking is affected too: its top three agree only
     0.63-0.75 across bootstraps.

8.6 A seed test hides it
  Across seeds the population ranking correlates 0.96-0.99 - exactly what the
  earlier draft reported and took as proof of consistency. The number was right.
  It measured the mildest possible disturbance.

8.7 The cause is the amount of data
  BRFSS cut down to smaller training sets, 30 bootstraps each, 26 independently
  drawn subsamples in total (10, 10, 5 and the full set):
     training rows            3,392    7,580    30,000   202,944
     events per predictor     15       34       135      910
     LR                       0.67     0.76     0.88     0.96
     MLP Expected Gradients   0.63     0.69     0.82     0.89
     MLP Integrated Gradients 0.58     0.65     0.81     0.88
     RF                       0.62     0.69     0.75     0.86
     XGB                      0.59     0.65     0.74     0.85
     test AUC (range)         0.83-0.84                  0.85
  Between the two smallest sizes the gain is 0.06-0.09 per model and every 95%
  interval excludes zero, although single subsamples overlap. At Framingham's
  ratio (34) BRFSS gives 0.65-0.76; Framingham itself gives 0.52-0.70.
  It was also checked that bootstrapping one sample does not exaggerate: drawing
  30 genuinely separate samples gave the same level.
  Meaning: the same pipeline on the same kind of data becomes stable simply by
  having more data, while AUC moves by about 0.02. Accuracy is not a warning light.

8.8 The model matters more than the explainer
  Same model, different explainer : 0.73 to 1.00
  Same explainer, different model : 0.51 to 0.93
  Per patient, agreement under an explainer swap is higher by 0.27 (Framingham) and
  0.24 (BRFSS). On BRFSS this is so although the four models differ by at most
  0.004 in AUC.
  Meaning: an explanation describes one fitted model, not the patient's disease.
  Two equally accurate models give the same patient different reasons.

8.9 Measurement noise, and the background sample
  Re-measuring a patient at published noise levels: agreement 0.67-0.85 on
  Framingham (RF lowest), 0.92-0.95 on BRFSS.
  Unexpected: simply re-drawing the 100 background rows, with nothing else changed,
  gave 0.80-0.93 (Framingham) and 0.80-0.85 (BRFSS). On BRFSS that is a larger
  disturbance than re-drawing the whole training set for four of the five
  model-explainer combinations. The background sample is
  usually treated as a technical detail.

8.10 Ranking the sources (share of variation due to real patient differences)
                                   Framingham   BRFSS
     model class                   0.67         0.84
     training sample (bootstrap)   0.68         0.98
     measurement noise             0.85         0.91
     background sample             0.96         0.96
     explainer                     0.96         1.00
     seed                          0.96         0.99
  Lower = bigger disturbance. On small data, model class and training sample are
  the two big ones. On large data, the training sample stops mattering and model
  class remains.

8.11 Our own settings, changed one after another
  Forest size (1,000 trees instead of 300), network width (128-64 instead of
  64-32), XGBoost learning rate: explanations move about as much as a seed change.
  Two things moved them more: different neural-network training settings, and a
  tree setting found by a wider search.
  Analysis settings (number of patients, number of bootstraps, top-1 to top-5,
  the overlap measure's parameter, number of noise draws, number of resamples):
  none changes a conclusion.

8.12 Which patients are unstable
  The best predictor is how close a patient's 3rd and 4th attributions are. The
  typical gap is only 1.5-3% of the patient's total attribution. Correlation with
  stability: 0.29-0.70 (Framingham), 0.76-0.84 (BRFSS).
  Meaning (the mechanism): when the third and fourth reasons are nearly tied, any
  small change in the model swaps them. Small training sets produce larger changes
  in the model. Being near the decision threshold was NOT a good predictor,
  contrary to what we expected.

8.13 SMOTE
  Mean predicted risk became 1.6 to 3.6 times the true rate; AUC did not improve;
  explanations changed more than between seeds. On Framingham, SMOTE put
  "education" into the top five features of the tree models, where it was absent
  without SMOTE.
  Meaning: the draft's odd finding about the random forest came from SMOTE, not
  from the explainer.

8.14 Replication under other master seeds
  Framingham under seeds 7, 11, 23, 101; BRFSS under seed 7. Each re-does the
  split, the patients, the background, the tuning and every seed.
                                       Framingham           BRFSS
                                       main    4 repl.      main        repl.
     bootstrap agreement               .52-.70 .51-.72      .84-.95     .81-.97
     seed agreement                    .82-.90 .77-.92      .90-.95     .92-.94
     faithfulness ratio                2.2-3.0 2.3-3.0      3.0-3.2     3.1-3.2
     plausibility                      .90-.96 .88-.97      -           -
     explainer swap minus model swap   0.27    0.20-0.30
  Held in every run: bootstrap below seed for every model; logistic regression the
  most stable model; model swap worse than explainer swap.
  Did not hold: "random forest is the least stable model" (it varies by run, so
  the claim was removed); Framingham AUC (0.70 in the main split, 0.73-0.75 in the
  others - the main split happened to be the least favourable); the neural
  network's exact figures (its early-stopping run chose 14, 30, 45, 64 epochs
  against 62).

8.15 Equally good hyperparameter settings
  Settings whose cross-validated loss is within one standard error of the best
  cannot honestly be told apart. Agreement between them:
                               Framingham   BRFSS
     LR                        0.96         1.00
     RF                        0.73         0.94
     XGB                       0.85         0.89
     MLP Integrated Gradients  0.76         0.87
     MLP Expected Gradients    0.84         0.94
  On small data this sits between a seed change and a re-drawn sample; on large
  data it is about the same as a re-drawn sample.
  For the neural network, three settings were varied in all eight combinations.
  Changing only weight decay gives 0.68-0.82; changing only the learning rate or
  only dropout gives 0.82-0.97. Weight decay is the one that matters.

10. THINGS THAT WENT WRONG, AND FINDINGS THAT GO AGAINST US
================================================================================

Software traps found (now reported in the paper as pitfalls for others)
  1. KernelSHAP default. The widely used shap library, by default, keeps only ten
     features and sets the rest to exactly zero. We noticed because the error
     against exact values did not shrink when given four times the computing
     budget. Everything using KernelSHAP was re-run with the setting off. Top-3
     results barely moved; lower-ranked features had been wiped out.
  2. Leak when re-tuning inside a bootstrap. A bootstrap sample contains copies of
     the same patient. Ordinary cross-validation puts copies on both sides of the
     split, which rewards over-complex models. It made XGBoost pick its largest
     setting every time and made its explanations look less stable than they are
     (0.44 instead of 0.585). Keeping all copies of a patient together fixed it.
  3. XGBoost with row/column subsampling is not reproducible across machines. Same
     data, version and seed gave different fitted models on Windows and Linux, and
     on Windows with 1, 4 or 12 threads. Without subsampling the fits were
     identical. The two fits behave like two seeds, so no conclusion depends on
     it, but the exact XGBoost numbers are tied to the machine.

Findings that weaken our own position (all stated in the paper)
  - The BRFSS neural network of the main analysis was slightly under-tuned: its
    weight decay lies outside the one-standard-error band, and a smaller value is
    better (AUC 0.849-0.850 against 0.848-0.849). The main analysis was not
    re-run, because the frozen plan fixed that recipe; the fact is reported.
  - The neural network's stability figures depend on its training recipe more than
    any other model's.
  - Framingham accuracy and calibration depend on the split.
  - BRFSS was replicated under only one other master seed (each run takes about 12
    hours).
  - On BRFSS the two variants of TreeSHAP agree with each other at only 0.61 for
    XGBoost, even though each is stable by itself.
  - Many analyses are post hoc: the sample-size experiment, the background-sample
    analysis, the near-tie analysis, all sensitivity and settings checks, the
    hyperparameter analysis and the replications.

Our own mistakes along the way (corrected)
  - Expected the risk equation to over-predict; it under-predicts (12.4% predicted
    against 14.8% observed). Cause not established.
  - Used a wrong figure for glucose variation (4.5%; the source says 5.6%).
  - Mapped two survey items to INTERHEART factors wrongly; removed after reading
    the paper.
  - Integrated Gradients was first run with too few steps (1.3% error); raised.
  - The tree explainer silently capped the background at 100 rows in one test.
  - Claimed "the ordering of models is the same on every split"; only "logistic
    regression is first" is true.
  - A Kaggle runner ran one stage in the wrong order; the two affected stages were
    re-run from the saved outputs.
  - One source (Andresen 2003) could not be read in full; its numbers were
    confirmed from a review that tabulates them, and we showed the remaining
    doubt (which statistic it reports) cannot affect our result.
  - A random-forest explanation error of up to 5% for a few patients was never
    traced. Declared.

11. WHAT THE PAPER CLAIMS, AND WHAT IT DOES NOT
================================================================================

Claims
  - For four standard models on two public heart-disease datasets, explanations
    are faithful and plausible, yet the order of a patient's leading features is
    not reproducible when the training data are re-drawn on a small dataset.
  - The instability follows the number of events per predictor and is invisible in
    AUC.
  - A seed-only stability check is too weak.
  - Model class changes explanations more than the choice among the attribution
    methods tested.
  - SMOTE harms calibration and alters explanations.
  - Recommendations for authors: report stability under re-drawn training data,
    per patient, with a chance level; report events per predictor; describe an
    explanation as a property of the fitted model; check calibration before
    explaining a rebalanced model; verify explainer settings on a case with a
    known answer; say how hyperparameters were chosen; group correlated features
    before comparing with clinical knowledge.

Does NOT claim
  - That it is the first study of its kind (the search was one database, titles
    only; two close 2026 neighbours were found and are cited).
  - That any model here is clinically valid.
  - That an unstable ordering would change a clinical decision. No clinician was
    involved, and the stability of the direction of each feature suggests that in
    some uses it would not.
  - Anything about explanation methods outside the SHAP / gradient family (LIME,
    rules, counterfactuals were not tested).
  - That the numbers transfer to hospital data.

================================================================================
12. LIMITS THAT MORE COMPUTING CANNOT REMOVE
================================================================================
  - Two public benchmark datasets; no hospital or registry cohort.
  - The Framingham file has no codebook and no HDL cholesterol.
  - BRFSS records existing, self-reported disease; some features are consequences
    of disease, not causes.
  - No clinical judgement of any explanation.
  - Only attribution methods of one family.
  - Sources of variation were examined one at a time; interactions between them
    are not estimated.
  - The plausibility reference comes from one publication and has six or seven
    groups.
  - The two noise models are not comparable in size.
  - Interventional attributions evaluate the model on feature combinations that
    may not occur in real patients.

================================================================================
13. WHAT THE WORK IS WORTH
================================================================================
  What is new is not any single ingredient - each has precedent - but the
  measurement: on the same patients, under one frozen plan, how much each source
  moves an individual explanation, tied to the amount of data, with the checks
  that make the measurement trustworthy.
  Who it is for: people who publish or review heart-disease prediction papers with
  SHAP plots, and people who build explanation features into clinical software.
  The practical message is short: a patient-level explanation from a model trained
  on a few thousand patients should be shown with a statement of how reproducible
  it is, and "it matches clinical knowledge" is not that statement.
  Realistic level: a careful methods audit suited to a medical-informatics or
  applied machine-learning journal. It is not a discovery and makes no claim to
  be. Its strength is that almost every objection a reviewer can raise has already
  been run and reported.
