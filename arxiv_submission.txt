TITLE
  Stability of feature-attribution explanations for heart-disease risk models: an
  audit across models, explainers, training samples and measurement noise

AUTHORS
  Yash Tyagi

ABSTRACT  (1,679 characters; arXiv's limit is 1,920. Paste as one paragraph.)
  Feature-attribution explanations such as SHAP are routinely attached to
  heart-disease prediction models and are usually supported by three checks: the
  model's discrimination, agreement of explanations across random seeds, and
  agreement with clinical knowledge. We audited whether those checks establish that
  an individual patient's explanation is reproducible. On two public datasets
  (Framingham teaching cohort, 4,240 people; BRFSS 2015, 253,680 respondents) we
  trained logistic regression, random forest, XGBoost and a small neural network,
  retrained each under 10 seeds and 50 to 100 bootstrap resamples, and explained the
  same 500 held-out patients each time with exact linear SHAP, TreeSHAP, Integrated
  Gradients, Expected Gradients and KernelSHAP, verified against exact Shapley
  values. The usual checks came out well: explanations were faithful, a patient's
  three leading features overlapped 0.82 to 0.95 across seeds, and grouped
  importance matched a published risk equation. Yet across bootstrap retrains on the
  small dataset the overlap was 0.52 to 0.70 (chance 0.13), an expected small-sample
  effect that discrimination did not reveal: subsampling the large dataset raised
  overlap from 0.58-0.67 to 0.85-0.96 while AUC moved from 0.83 to 0.85. On both
  datasets, swapping the model class changed a patient's leading features more than
  swapping the explainer, and hyperparameter settings that cross-validation could not
  tell apart gave overlap of 0.73 to 1.00. The sign of leading attributions was
  stable. Results held under five further master seeds. We report three software
  pitfalls and recommend reporting per-patient stability under resampling of the
  training data.
